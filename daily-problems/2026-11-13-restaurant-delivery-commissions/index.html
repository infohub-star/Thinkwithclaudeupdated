Why do small restaurants in India keep so little of each food delivery order?

Commissions, unapproved discounts and ad fees can eat 40-60% of a delivery order, and most owners cannot see exactly where the money went. Here's "PayoutLens", a payout auditor one person could build with Claude in a weekend.

Written by Shantanu Anand, AI Trainer & Consultant · 13 November 2026

Food & RestaurantsItch Score 78Build time: a weekend

The problem

Who feels it: Owners of small and mid-sized restaurants, cafes and cloud kitchens who depend on delivery apps for a large share of sales. They rarely have an accountant who reads every weekly payout statement, so deductions they never agreed to go unnoticed.

  • Where this came from: Indian news coverage of the 2026 Bengaluru restaurant protest against delivery platforms (The Week, The News Minute), the long-running restaurant association competition case (Business Standard), and startup coverage of direct-ordering tools (Inc42).
  • The cut is large. Restaurant owners in Bengaluru report commissions of 20-30% per order, and an NRAI Bengaluru representative says commission plus discount contributions can take 40-60% (The News Minute).
  • A Rs 1 lakh order month can pay out Rs 40,000-45,000 after ads, long-distance levies, collection charges and commission on GST, according to the Bangalore Hotels Association (The News Minute).
  • Owners want transparency first. In July 2026 the Bruhat Bengaluru Hotels Association demanded a detailed breakdown of commissions, charges and payouts, and consent before discounts and ad charges (The Week).
  • Platforms conceded some of it. Restaurant associations say Zomato agreed to make restaurant-funded promotions opt-in and review past wrongful deductions, and Swiggy agreed to refund campaigns run without approval (The News Minute).
  • Delivery matters too much to quit. Restaurants attribute about one-third of revenue to these platforms, per a JM Financial report cited by Inc42 (Inc42).

Who's already working on it

Direct-ordering and zero-commission tools such as uEngage, Thrive, DotPe, Reelo and Bitsila help restaurants build their own ordering channel, and ONDC offers an open-network alternative (Inc42, YourStory). These focus on new orders. Few tools sit on the restaurant's side and audit the payouts from existing platforms line by line.

The solution idea

"PayoutLens" reads a restaurant's weekly delivery-app payout statements, shows the true take rate per order, flags promotions and charges the owner never approved, and drafts the refund claim.

Statement importUpload payout PDFs or Excel exports from each delivery app; it extracts every order, fee and deduction.
True take rateShows what share of each order the restaurant actually keeps, by week, by dish and by app.
Unapproved charge alertsFlags discounts, ad fees and penalties that do not match a campaign the owner approved.
Refund claim draftsWrites a clear, itemised dispute for each flagged charge, ready to paste into the partner portal.
Direct-order nudgeIdentifies the dishes and customers where moving to direct ordering would save the most.

Weekend build plan

  1. Saturday morning — evidence: Collect anonymised payout statements from three restaurants and list every fee type you see.
  2. Saturday midday — parser: Use Claude Code to build parsers for the two most common statement formats.
  3. Saturday afternoon — rules: Write the take-rate maths and the approved-campaign rules as a Claude skill and test them.
  4. Saturday evening — alerts: Add the unapproved-charge check and the refund claim draft.
  5. Sunday morning — report: Build the weekly report artifact and a simple dashboard.
  6. Sunday midday — automation: Add a scheduled task that reads new statements from email every Monday.
  7. Sunday afternoon — pilot: Run it on real statements from the three restaurants and compare with their own numbers.

Starter prompt for Claude Code

Build a Next.js + TypeScript web app called "PayoutLens" for small Indian restaurants.

Flow: the owner uploads weekly payout statements (PDF or Excel) from food delivery apps -> extract every order line, commission, GST, discount share, ad fee, penalty and net payout -> call the Claude API to map unfamiliar line items to standard fee categories -> compute the true take rate per order, per week and per app -> compare discount and ad deductions against a list of campaigns the owner approved, and flag mismatches -> draft an itemised refund claim for each flag.

Include: a dashboard with take rate over time, a flagged-charges table, a downloadable weekly PDF report, sample anonymised statements for tests, and unit tests for the fee maths. Keep all restaurant data private per account.

Riskiest assumption, and how to test it

Assumption: Platforms may already refund wrongly applied charges quickly enough that owners do not feel the need for an audit tool.

Test first: Audit eight weeks of statements for five restaurants for free and count how much in disputable charges you find. If at least three restaurants have charges worth more than a month's subscription, the product has a case; if not, lead with the take-rate and direct-order insights instead.

Read each platform's partner agreement before automating anything inside their portals, and keep disputes factual. Handle restaurant and customer data under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act.

My take

Most owners already know delivery apps are expensive; what they lack is a line-by-line view of where the money goes. Claude is good at turning messy statements into a clear, factual list, which is exactly what an owner needs before negotiating or disputing. Start with the audit and the true take rate, then add direct ordering advice.
