AI Agents Explained for Non-Technical Managers

By Shantanu Anand · 6 November 2026

Your leadership team wants an "AI agent plan". A vendor says their agent can run a whole department, and someone in your team has already connected one to their inbox. You are expected to have a view, but nobody has explained what an agent actually is.

You don't need a technical background to make good decisions here. You need a clear definition, a way to spot suitable work and a few rules for keeping people in charge.

What an AI agent is

An AI agent is software that takes a goal, plans the steps, uses tools to carry them out and adjusts as it goes. A tool here means anything the agent can use on your behalf, such as your files, email, calendar or a web browser.

A chatbot mostly gives you an answer, and you do the next step yourself. An agent can do some of those next steps for you.

Say you need a summary of this week's customer complaints.

  • With a chatbot, you export the complaints, paste them in, ask for a summary, copy the result into a document and send it.
  • With an agent, you describe the outcome. It opens the export, groups the complaints, drafts the document and stops for your review before anything is sent.

The easiest comparison is a capable new hire. You wouldn't hand a new hire the company chequebook on day one. You'd give clear instructions and limited access, then check the work closely until they had earned more trust. Treat an agent the same way, with one difference: it has no judgment or accountability of its own, so responsibility stays with the people using it.

How an agent works, in four steps

Most agents follow the same basic loop:

  1. Understand the goal. You describe the result you want and any limits.
  2. Make a plan. It breaks the goal into smaller steps.
  3. Take action. It uses the tools and data you have allowed.
  4. Check and adjust. It looks at what happened, then continues, changes course or asks you a question.

The risk sits in step three. A wrong answer in a chat window stays on your screen until someone acts on it. A wrong action can reach a client, change a record or delete a file. That is why access and approvals matter more than the model's cleverness.

Where the three main tools stand

All three companies now offer agent features, though names and access change often. This is the picture as of November 2026, so check what your own plan includes.

  • ChatGPT: OpenAI launched workspace agents as a research preview for its Business, Enterprise, Edu and Teachers plans. Teams can build shared agents and use them in ChatGPT and Slack. For sensitive steps, such as sending an email, you can require the agent to ask for permission first.
  • Claude: Anthropic built Cowork for longer tasks and has been merging it with chat into one Claude, rolling out plan by plan. You can choose whether Claude asks before acting or runs on its own and checks in when needed. Anthropic also states that you remain responsible for the actions Claude takes on your behalf.
  • Gemini: Google Cloud announced Gemini agent in October. It was reported as a private preview for select Gemini Enterprise subscribers, so many teams won't have it yet.

Don't choose based on this list alone. Your company's approved tools and data rules come first, and a 30-minute test on real work will tell you more than any feature page.

Good first tasks for an agent

Look for work that is repetitive, follows clear steps and is easy to check. Your team should already know what a good result looks like.

  • Weekly reports: A sales manager in Pune gets last week's numbers pulled into the usual format, with changes flagged for her review.
  • Meeting follow-ups: A project lead in Gurugram gets a draft list of action points and owners, which he checks before sharing.
  • Research packs: An L&D head in Bengaluru gets a comparison of training options from documents she has chosen, with sources she can verify.
  • File tidying: An office administrator in Chennai gets scattered invoices renamed and sorted into folders, working on a copy first.

What to keep with a person

Some work needs a named human owner, however good the tool becomes.

  • Anything sent to clients, regulators or the public
  • Payments, purchases and contract commitments
  • Hiring, firing, appraisals and other decisions about people
  • Medical, legal and compliance judgments
  • Deleting or overwriting records that matter

An agent can still help with the preparation. It can draft the client email or organise the appraisal notes. A person reviews the work and makes the final call.

The risk most managers don't hear about

Agents read a lot of outside content, and that creates a problem called prompt injection. It means someone hides instructions in a web page, email or document, and the agent may follow them as if they came from you.

Picture an agent reading supplier emails. One email contains a hidden line telling it to forward your customer list to an outside address. If the agent can both read that email and send messages, it has what the attack needs. Anthropic says its safeguards reduce this risk but can't remove it, and OpenAI compares these attacks to phishing.

So limit what an agent can read and what it can do, especially when it handles outside content. Require approval before anything leaves the company.

Five questions before you say yes

Ask these about any agent, whether it comes from a vendor or someone in your team.

  1. What can it read? Name the exact folders, inboxes and systems.
  2. What can it change or send? Start with drafts only where you can.
  3. When does it stop and ask? Get specific about which actions need approval.
  4. Can we see what it did? You want a record you can review, along with a way to undo mistakes.
  5. Who owns the result? Put one person's name against it.

You can use this prompt to turn a real task into a clear brief:

Role: You are a practical AI adviser helping a non-technical manager at an
Indian company.

Ask: Read the task below. Tell me if it is a good fit for an AI agent, a
regular chatbot or a person. If an agent fits, list the steps it would take,
the access it needs, and the points where a person must approve the work.
Flag the biggest risk and how we would catch a mistake.

Context: Task: [describe the task]. How often we do it: [frequency]. Tools and
data involved: [list, without confidential details]. Who sees the result:
[audience]. Cost of a mistake: [consequence].

Tone: Plain and direct. No jargon. Tell me if an agent is overkill.

Treat the answer as a starting point. You know your team, your data rules and what a mistake would really cost.

FAQs

Is an AI agent the same as a chatbot?

No. A chatbot mainly responds to you in conversation. An agent can also plan steps and use tools to carry out work, within the access you give it.

Do I need to know coding to use one?

Not for many current tools, which accept instructions in plain English. You do need clear instructions, sensible access limits and help from IT on company systems.

Will agents replace my team?

They can take over some repetitive tasks, but they still need people to set goals, check work and own decisions. Start with one task and judge the results yourself.

Try this today

  1. Pick one repetitive task your team does every week, and write down what a good result looks like.
  2. Run it through the prompt above, leaving out confidential details.
  3. Answer the five questions for that task, then check with IT before giving any agent access.
