You have an idea that keeps you up at night. Maybe it is a meal-prep service for working parents in Pune, or a training programme for clinic receptionists. Testing it properly feels expensive: a website, months of ads, maybe a small team.

Most people either spend too much too early or never start at all. There is a cheaper first step. You can use AI to pressure-test the idea in an afternoon, so you only put real money behind ideas that survive the questions.

One honest warning before we start. AI can help you think, research and spot gaps. It cannot tell you whether people will pay. Only talking to real customers can do that. Treat everything below as homework before those conversations, not a replacement for them.

## Why a reusable assistant beats a one-off chat

Typing "Is this a good business idea?" into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini gets you a polite, generic answer.

A better approach is to set up a small, reusable assistant once and use it for every idea you have. All three tools let you do this:

- **ChatGPT:** Custom GPTs, or Projects with saved instructions.
- **Claude:** Projects, where you add instructions and reference files.
- **Gemini:** Gems, which are saved assistants with their own instructions.

The benefit is consistency. Every idea goes through the same tough questions in the same order. After five ideas, you can compare them fairly instead of falling for whichever one you felt most excited about that day.

## Step 1: Write the instructions once

Here is a copy-paste setup in the RACT style (Role, Ask, Context, Tone). Paste it into the instructions box of your Custom GPT, Claude Project or Gemini Gem.

```
Role: You are a sceptical but fair business analyst who helps
first-time founders in India test ideas before they spend money.

Ask: When I describe a business idea, work through these steps
in order and wait for my reply after each one:
1. Restate the idea in one sentence: who the customer is, what
   problem it solves, and how it makes money.
2. List the 5 biggest assumptions the idea depends on. Rank them
   from riskiest to safest.
3. Search the web for existing competitors and alternatives,
   including the "do nothing" option. Give links for every
   competitor you name. Say clearly if you could not find any.
4. List the main risks: demand, pricing, operations, regulation
   and timing.
5. Suggest 3 cheap tests I can run in under two weeks to check
   the riskiest assumption.
6. Write 8 open-ended questions I can ask 10 potential customers.

Context: I am testing ideas on a small budget. I want to find
reasons an idea might fail before I invest. Do not invent
statistics, market sizes or company names. If you are unsure,
say so and tell me what I should check myself.

Tone: Direct, plain English, no hype. Challenge me politely when
my reasoning is weak.
```

Make sure web search is switched on in whichever tool you use. All three can search the web, and each offers a deeper research mode for longer reports. Without search, the assistant is guessing about competitors from its training data, which may be out of date.

## Step 2: Run your idea through it

Take a real example. Ritu runs HR for a mid-sized company in Bengaluru and wants to start a side business: a weekend workshop that teaches new team leads how to give feedback.

She opens her assistant and writes three or four sentences about the idea, who it is for and how she plans to charge. The assistant comes back with its one-sentence summary. If the summary sounds vague, the idea probably is too.

Next come the assumptions. For Ritu, the list might include:

- New team leads feel they need feedback training.
- Companies will pay for it, or individuals will pay themselves.
- People will give up a weekend for it.
- She can fill a room without paid ads.

Notice that the riskiest assumption is often not the one you were worried about. Ritu was worried about the content. The bigger risk was whether anyone would give up a Saturday.

## Step 3: Check competitors properly

This is where AI saves the most time. In minutes it can find training firms, online courses and free content already covering the topic.

Two rules here:

1. **Click every link.** AI tools can still get details wrong or point to pages that have changed. If a competitor matters to your decision, visit their site yourself.
2. **Do not panic about competition.** Competitors are often proof that people pay for this. The useful question is what they do badly, or which customers they ignore.

Ask a follow-up like: "Based on these competitors, which customer group seems underserved? What would make my version clearly different?"

## Step 4: Look hard at the risks

Founders often skip this uncomfortable part. The assistant has no emotional attachment to your idea, which is exactly why it helps.

Ask it to argue the opposite case:

```
Role: Act as an investor who has decided NOT to fund this idea.

Ask: Write the 5 strongest reasons you are saying no, and for
each one, tell me what evidence would change your mind.

Context: Use the idea and research from this conversation.
Be specific to the Indian market where relevant.

Tone: Blunt but respectful. No softening.
```

You do not need to accept every objection. But if you cannot answer one of them, write it down. That becomes something to test.

## Step 5: Design cheap tests

The goal is to learn the most for the least money. Good tests at this stage include:

- **A simple landing page** describing the offer, with a "register interest" form. Share it in a few relevant WhatsApp or LinkedIn groups.
- **A pre-sale or waitlist,** where people commit before you build anything.
- **A manual version.** Before building an app, do the service by hand for three customers and see what they actually value.

Ask your assistant to define what "success" looks like before you run each test. For example: "If fewer than 20 people register in two weeks, I will rethink the audience." Setting the bar in advance stops you from explaining away weak results.

## Step 6: Talk to real customers

This is the step AI cannot do for you. Use the 8 questions from Step 1, and speak to at least ten people who match your target customer.

Ask about their past behaviour, not their future intentions. "When did you last pay for training like this?" tells you far more than "Would you attend my workshop?"

After the calls, paste your notes into the assistant and ask: "What patterns do you see? Which of my assumptions did these conversations support, and which did they weaken?" Remove names and personal details first.

## What AI should and should not decide

AI is good at speed, structure and finding things you missed. It is weak at judging real demand, local relationships and your own ability to execute.

So use it to inform the decision, not to make it. The final call should rest on what customers said and did, plus your own judgement about whether you want to spend the next two years on this.

## FAQs

**Can AI tell me if my business idea will succeed?**
No. It can highlight risks, competitors and weak assumptions, but only real customers, through their actions, can show whether there is demand.

**Which tool is best for testing business ideas: ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini?**
All three work well for this. Pick the one you already use, set up a reusable assistant, and make sure web search is turned on.

**Is it safe to share my business idea with an AI tool?**
Avoid pasting confidential details, customer data or anything under an agreement. Check your tool's data settings, and use a business or team plan if your company provides one.
